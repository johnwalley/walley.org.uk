Almost two thirds of the movement on a Formula 1 lap chart isn't overtaking. Across this season's 20 races there were 1,030 passes on track, and 1,865 places changed by pit stops and safety cars. On the chart they all look the same.

I've been drawing bumps charts, the rowing version of the same idea, for well over a decade. I built [Cambridge Bumps](https://www.cambridgebumps.com/) around them. I'm about to start a new job in F1, so I wanted to see what carries over.

## Bumps charts only move when someone is caught

In a bumps race, boats set off in a line and try to catch the crew in front. A crew that catches the one ahead has made a *bump*, and the two swap places for the next day's racing. A bumps chart plots that order day by day, one line per crew.

A crew only changes position by bumping the crew ahead or being bumped by the crew behind. There are no pit stops or safety cars to shuffle the order without anyone catching anyone. So wherever two lines cross, one crew caught another. A flat line means the crew rowed over, finishing the course without catching or being caught.

F1's version is called a lap chart, with each driver's position plotted lap by lap. It has many more columns, one per lap instead of one per day, but the question is the same. When a line moves, did someone actually race and overtake for that place?

At this year's Italian Grand Prix, mostly yes. A red flag on lap 3 stopped the race, and every car still running changed tyres while they waited. After that, the order was largely decided on track: 107 passes and only 30 places changed by pit stops. Follow Kimi Antonelli. He starts 19th and leads by lap 18. His only proper pit stop drops him to sixth, and he works his way back to win.

## Overtaking in the pit lane

Monza turns out to be the exception. For all 20 races, I compared the order at the end of every lap with the lap before. Every time two drivers swapped places, I checked whether either of them had pitted on that lap or the one before. If so, the swap counts as a pit stop, not a pass.

A pit stop costs about 24 seconds in the pit lane, which is several places on a crowded track. The driver drops down the order, then climbs back up as the cars ahead make their own stops. Nobody has overtaken anyone, but on the chart it looks exactly as if they have.

The Bahrain Grand Prix is a good example. Not the one in Bahrain. This year's race moved to Sepang, outside Kuala Lumpur. Rain delayed the start by an hour and a half, and the first two laps were run behind the safety car before a standing start. After that came two more safety cars, a virtual safety car and 73 pit stops in 55 laps. That's 102 passes and 238 places changed in the pits.

## Marking pit stops adds clutter

My first idea was to mark every pit stop. Here's the same race with a dot at the end of every lap where a driver came in.

It doesn't help. When the safety car comes out and 18 of the 22 cars pit on the same lap, you get a column of dots telling you what you already knew. The dots also only mark the driver who stopped. If they drop five places, the five cars they fall behind move up, and those lines get no dot.

## Fading out the pit stops

Something that worked reasonably well was changing how each step of a line is drawn. If a driver's change of position on a lap came only from pit stops, whether their own or other people's, that step is drawn faded and dotted. Real passes remain solid. A step fades when every swap the driver was part of on that lap involved a car that had pitted on that lap or the one before. If even one swap was a pass, then the step stays solid. A step also fades when a driver moves up only because a car ahead of them retired.

If we follow Charles Leclerc, we can see him start fourth before an early stop on lap 3 drops him to 22nd. That drop fades out, and what's left is a solid line climbing back through the field. He stops three more times, and those drops fade too, along with the places he picks up while other drivers stop.

### Team colours repeat, so second cars are dashed

Lines are coloured by team, which is the F1 convention but awkward for a chart. There are eleven teams with two cars each, so every colour appears twice. Some teams are close enough to confuse anyway: Ferrari and Audi are both red, Cadillac and Haas are both grey, and four teams use blue. Each team's second car is drawn dashed, and every line is labelled at both ends. Hover over or tap a line to pick it out from the rest.

## Where the rule gets it wrong

The pit stop rule only looks at who pitted and when. To check it, I looked at how much the gap between the two cars changed on each swap.

When only one of the two cars had pitted, the gap between them usually moved by ten seconds or more, which is what you'd expect from a stop (1,273 of 1,656 swaps). In 202 swaps it moved by less than five seconds. Almost all of those (186) were on the lap the car came in, where it loses a few seconds slowing down for the pit lane. Only 16 were on the lap it came out, where a car on cold tyres might really have been passed. That's close enough.

Retirements were a bigger problem. When a car retires, everyone behind it moves up a place without passing anyone. There's no swap to classify, so those steps stayed solid and looked like passes. There were 207 of them after the first lap this season, and they're now faded too.

There are two problems I can't fix with this data:

- **Anything between laps.** Positions are taken once a lap, at the line. A pass and a re-pass within the same lap don't show up at all.
- **The first lap.** The 469 changes of place between the grid and the end of lap 1 aren't classified, and they're not in any of the totals above.

## Check what moves the line

A rank chart only works if every move on it means something happened. In bumps racing that's always true, so the chart stands by itself. In F1 it's less clear cut. At Monza the lines moved because of overtakes, but that was true in only five of this season's 20 races. In the other 15, pit stops moved more places than passes did, so the chart shows strategy more than racing.

Showing why each line moved was enough to make the bumps chart useful again.

## About the data

The data comes from [OpenF1](https://openf1.org), an unofficial, community-run API that isn't associated with Formula 1. Positions are the order cars crossed the line at the end of each lap, so they don't include penalties applied after the race. OpenF1's lap start times are occasionally out by tens of seconds for a single lap, which shows up as a car dropping to the back and returning a lap later. Using lap start times alone, that happened four times at Suzuka. To smooth it out, each crossing time is the median of three estimates: the next lap's start, this lap's start plus its duration, and the previous crossing plus this lap's duration.
